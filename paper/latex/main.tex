\begin{abstract}
People at the margins, frontline practitioners, and communities often know a problem before
researchers do, because they live with it every day. Turning what they know into ``knowledge''
has historically run into walls of language, method, funding, institutions, and expertise. AI
lowers those walls quickly, but a new problem follows: now anyone can produce text that looks
smart and academic, without it being clear which part is evidence, which part is interpretation,
and which part AI is smoothing over past what the data actually supports. This paper reports
\textbf{glosa} (\emph{Rigour Without Infrastructure}), a Standalone Scholar methodology for
human--AI knowledge co-production: a person with no university, no lab, and no research team
needs a mechanism that forces every claim to answer, for one claim at a time, what was actually
seen, what the data separates, what AI filled in, what was assumed, and whether it has met
independent evidence or objection. The paper is itself an instance of the method it describes: it
is genre-routed as \texttt{design\_science} (Problem--Objectives--Design--Demonstration--
Evaluation), reaches tier \DrTier{} on its design claims and \FiniteDiag{} on one mechanically
executed check (its own LaTeX build), and states plainly what was executed versus what remains
specified. Its single most important limitation is structural, not incidental: every evidence
relation behind its own ten claims is independence class $I0$ (same-session self-read), so its own
knowledge state is \texttt{K0} by its own rule, not \texttt{K1} — a reflexive finding, not an
exception.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
A person with no university, no lab, and no research team can, today, ask an AI system to help
them investigate a problem they live with, and receive back a fluent, structured, citation-shaped
document. What that document does \emph{not} come with, by default, is any mechanical way for a
reader --- or the author themselves, a week later --- to tell which sentence rests on something
actually observed, which rests on an assumption, which rests on the AI's own training prior, and
which has ever been checked by anyone other than the same session that produced it. \textbf{glosa}
is a design response to exactly this gap: not a claim that rigour has been achieved, but a
specification, partially demonstrated, of a mechanism (the claim card, \Cref{sec:method}) that
makes those distinctions answerable in the first place. Every load-bearing claim in this
introduction and below carries a claim-card id (\claimref{Cn}) resolving in
\Cref{sec:claim-matrix}.
